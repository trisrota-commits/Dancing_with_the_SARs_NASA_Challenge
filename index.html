NISAR radar

Teal frames: the newest are bold, and they fade over four days.

Every place NISAR images, land and ice, from its passes of the last 24 days; the newest pass over each place lies on top. NASA publishes passes up to two weeks late, so the newest days are thin and the last complete round is 12 to 24 days old. It takes longer to load.

  • Green: vegetation
  • Orange or yellow: towns and thinly vegetated ground
  • Blue: calm water, dry sand, frozen ground
  • Red: rough water
NASA's colours for this map layer, as its archive describes them (ASF, NISAR in Worldview). The single radar pictures shown for an event use NASA's other rendering, magenta and green, explained in the key beside them. Why whole strips differ. Each pass is a strip, and NASA colours each one on its own, so neighbours meet at a hard edge. A whole strip in orange beside green ones was recorded in one radar channel only: without the second channel, which leaves and branches send back, forest shows orange too. (Our finding, checked against NASA's catalogue over the Amazon.)
Events on the map

  • Colour: the kind of event (the buttons above). Point at a dot to read it.
  • Bigger dot, taller beam: a stronger event
  • Teal ring: NISAR imaged that spot in its latest passes
  • White ring: the one you selected
  • Quick spreading rings: it arrived in the last minutes
  • One soft ring every few seconds: first reported in the last 24 hours
  • Line through a dot: the path it has taken

      Earth's surface never holds still.

      Every 12 days the NISAR radar satellite images the planet's land and ice again, through cloud and at night. This globe shows what it sees change, next to the events being reported on the ground right now. The coloured strips on the globe are its radar passes of the last 12 days.
